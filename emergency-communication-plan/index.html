When the call will not go through, a plan made in advance is how your family finds each other.

An emergency communication plan is a written, practiced arrangement for how a household stays in contact and reunites when a disaster strikes and normal phone service is overwhelmed or down. It typically names an out-of-area contact everyone calls or texts to relay status, designates rally points near home and outside the neighborhood, records key numbers and medical details on paper cards, and sets a PACE plan of primary, alternate, contingency, and emergency methods. Frequently asked questions

Why name an out-of-town emergency contact?

During a local disaster, calls within the affected area often fail while long-distance connections still work, and a text may go through when a voice call will not. Picking one out-of-area friend or relative gives every family member a single person to check in with, who then relays everyone status and location. It turns a scattered family into a hub-and-spoke network that works even when local lines are jammed.

What is a PACE plan for communication?

PACE stands for primary, alternate, contingency, and emergency, and it means lining up four ways to communicate so no single failure leaves you cut off. Your primary might be cell phones, your alternate text messaging, your contingency a family radio or GMRS handheld, and your emergency a physical message left at a rally point. The guide shows how to build a PACE plan matched to your family and area.

Where should our family rally points be?

Choose at least two meeting places: one right outside your home for a sudden emergency like a fire, and one outside the neighborhood in case the area is evacuated or inaccessible. Some families add a third, out-of-town location for a wide-area disaster. Everyone should know the addresses, how to get there without a phone or GPS, and how long to wait before moving to the next point.

What should go on a printed emergency contact card?

Each family member should carry a wallet-sized card listing the out-of-area contact, key family cell numbers, home and work or school addresses, and any critical medical information such as allergies or medications. Because phones die, break, or lock, the card ensures a child or a stranger helping them can still make contact. The guide includes a fillable template you can print and laminate for each person.
