Survival Garden Planning for Food and Storage

A survival garden prioritizes household food needs, local growing conditions and storage. Potatoes, dry beans, squash and other staples can contribute calories, but yields vary with climate, soil, pests and experience.

Work backward from household consumption, trial crops and record actual yields. Keep other food sources and plan for losses.

Frequently asked questions

How big does a survival garden need to be to feed a family?

There is no universal acreage that will feed a family. Use local yield estimates, dietary needs, growing season, irrigation and storage losses to plan, then adjust based on actual harvests.

What should I grow in a survival garden?

Choose regionally suitable staple crops and a variety of vegetables for nutrition. Storage conditions differ by crop; follow crop-specific guidance rather than assuming all produce keeps for months.

How do I plan how much of each crop to plant per person?

Start from weekly consumption and multiply across the year, so five pounds of potatoes a week becomes well over two hundred pounds a year, then plant somewhat more than that to absorb losses to pests, disease, and weather.

What is succession planting and why does it matter for a survival garden?

Succession planting means replanting a bed the moment one crop finishes rather than letting it sit idle, and staggering early, mid, and late varieties so the harvest arrives steadily instead of all at once. For a survival garden it roughly multiplies what a fixed plot can produce in a season and spreads both the labor and the food supply across months, which cuts down on spoilage gluts and hungry gaps alike.
