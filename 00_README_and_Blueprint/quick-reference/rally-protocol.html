If your phones die, does everyone still know where to go?

A family reconnection plan has four parts: a primary rally point everyone can reach on foot, a secondary rally point outside the immediate area for when the primary isn't safe, an out-of-area contact that separated family members can call (long-distance lines often work when local ones are jammed), and a fixed check-in schedule. Agree on all four before you need them — not while you're separated.

Build your plan

Primary rally point

A specific, memorized physical location near home — not just an address. "The oak tree at the end of Miller Road" survives a power outage; a street address you half-remember doesn't.

Secondary rally point

Somewhere outside your immediate neighborhood — a relative's house, a specific mile marker — for when the primary location isn't accessible or safe.

Out-of-area contact

A friend or relative outside the disaster region. Local phone lines often jam during a crisis while long-distance and out-of-state calls still connect — everyone calls this person to relay their status and location.

Check-in schedule

Agree on a recurring window — for example, every 3 hours on the hour — to attempt to reconnect if separated, so no one is guessing when to try again.

Kids and dependents

Give any dependent who might be separated from a caregiver a laminated card with the rally point, the out-of-area contact's number, and the basic plan in plain language. It doesn't rely on a phone, a memory under stress, or anyone else being nearby to help.

Frequently asked questions

Why use an out-of-area contact for family emergencies?

During many local disasters, local phone lines and cell towers get overloaded, but long-distance or out-of-state calls often still connect. A relative or friend outside the affected region can act as a relay point everyone calls to check in and share their status and location.

What makes a good family rally point?

A specific, memorized physical location rather than just an address — street signs, GPS, and landmarks can be unusable or destroyed. "The oak tree at the end of Miller Road" works better under stress than a number you have to recall correctly.

Should kids carry emergency contact information?

Yes. A laminated card with the rally point, the out-of-area contact's phone number, and the basic family meeting plan is a simple, durable backup for a dependent who may not have a phone or may be separated from caregivers.
