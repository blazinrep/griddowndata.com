Frequently asked questions

Do I actually need a Faraday bag for a USB drive?

A USB drive has no antenna and can't be remotely tracked or hacked over a network, so a Faraday bag isn't protecting it from signals. What it protects against is EMP and solar flare (CME) events, which can induce enough voltage in exposed circuitry to corrupt or destroy flash storage. If the drive is part of an emergency kit meant to survive a grid-down event, shielding it is cheap insurance.

What's the difference between a cheap $10 faraday pouch and a $50+ one?

Shielding effectiveness (measured in dB of attenuation), fabric quality across all sides (not just the flap), and seam construction. Sub-$10 pouches are frequently RFID-only — they'll stop a key fob relay attack but won't meaningfully attenuate an EMP pulse. Brands that publish independently tested dB ratings, like Mission Darkness, are the ones worth trusting for EMP-grade protection.

Does a Faraday bag need to be airtight to work?

No, but the seal matters. Faraday shielding works by conducting current around the enclosed space rather than through it, which requires continuous conductive contact around every opening. A bag with a proper fold-and-seal or welded closure blocks signal; a loosely rolled top or gapped zipper leaks it.

Can I make my own Faraday bag instead of buying one?

Yes — conductive fabric (like Mission Darkness's own TitanRF fabric kits) can be sewn into a DIY pouch, and it's a common approach in the DIY prepper community. It's cheaper per square foot, but you lose the lab-tested shielding numbers and welded-seam construction that branded bags provide, so effectiveness varies more with your build quality.
