When the AC dies in a heat wave, telling heat exhaustion from heat stroke is a life-or-death call.

Heat-related illness is a progression of conditions caused by the body overheating, running from heat cramps and heat exhaustion up to life-threatening heat stroke. Heat exhaustion brings heavy sweating, weakness, nausea, and cool clammy skin, and is treated by moving to shade, resting, loosening clothing, applying cool cloths, and sipping water. Heat stroke is a medical emergency marked by confusion, a body temperature around 103 degrees Fahrenheit or higher, and hot skin that may be dry or sweaty; it demands rapid cooling and emergency care. Frequently asked questions

How do I tell heat exhaustion from heat stroke?

Heat exhaustion leaves a person sweating heavily with cool, clammy, pale skin, feeling weak, dizzy, and nauseated but still mentally clear. Heat stroke is far more dangerous: the person becomes confused, agitated, or unconscious, their body temperature climbs to around 103 degrees Fahrenheit or higher, and their skin may be hot and either dry or still sweating. Confusion is the red flag that turns it into an emergency.

What is the first aid for heat stroke?

Heat stroke is a medical emergency, so call for emergency help immediately, then cool the person as fast as possible while you wait. Move them into shade or a cool space, remove excess clothing, and lower their temperature with a cold-water or ice bath if available, or by wetting the skin and fanning and placing cold wet cloths on the neck, armpits, and groin.

How do I treat heat exhaustion before it gets worse?

Get the person out of the heat into shade or a cooler area, have them rest, and loosen or remove unnecessary clothing. Apply cool wet cloths or a cool shower and give frequent small sips of cool water or an electrolyte drink. Most people recover with rest and fluids, but if symptoms worsen or last beyond an hour, or vomiting begins, seek medical care.

How can I prevent heat illness when there is no air conditioning?

Drink water steadily before you feel thirsty, and add electrolytes during heavy sweating, since plain water alone can fall short. Work during the cooler morning and evening hours, rest often in shade, and wear light, loose, light-colored clothing. Wetting your skin and clothing, improvising cross-ventilation, and moving to the lowest, shadiest part of the home all help shed heat when the grid is down.
