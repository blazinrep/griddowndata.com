Frequently asked questions

Why does my well stop working in a power outage?

Most modern wells use an electric submersible pump sitting deep in the water, so when the grid goes down the pump stops and no water reaches the house. This is why preparedness planning treats a manual or solar backup as essential: the well itself still holds water, but without power you have no way to lift it until you add a hand pump or alternative pumping method.

How deep can a hand pump lift water?

It depends on the design. Simple suction or pitcher pumps are limited to about 25 feet at sea level because atmospheric pressure can only push water up so far. Deep-well rod-and-cylinder pumps place the piston down in the water column and physically push water upward, reaching static water levels of a couple hundred feet or more. The guide explains matching pump type to your well's measured depth.

Can I add a hand pump to my existing electric well?

Yes, and it is a common resilience upgrade. Many deep-well hand pumps are engineered to install alongside an existing electric submersible pump in the same well casing, giving you a manual backup without abandoning your everyday electric system. The guide covers casing clearance, sanitary well-cap considerations, and how the two pumps coexist so a blackout no longer means no running water in your home.

What are low-tech ways to get water from a well?

Beyond manufactured pumps, a well bucket, a long narrow cylinder with a one-way valve in its base, can be lowered on a rope to retrieve water from almost any depth, and solar-powered pumps can run whenever the sun shines. Each option trades off speed, cost, and reliability. The guide compares hand pumps, bucket-and-rope, and solar so you can build layered water access rather than a single point of failure.
