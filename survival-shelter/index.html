Frequently asked questions

Why is shelter the first survival priority?

In wind, rain, or cold, your body loses heat far faster than most people expect, and hypothermia can set in within hours, long before dehydration or hunger become dangerous. That is why the common rule of threes puts exposure near the top: in harsh conditions you may have only about three hours before the cold becomes life-threatening. Getting out of the elements protects the core temperature that everything else depends on.

What makes a good shelter site?

Site selection matters as much as the shelter itself. You want flat, dry ground above any low spot where cold air and water pool, with natural windbreaks such as trees or terrain, and no dead limbs overhead that could fall in a storm. Staying off the cold ground is critical, because conduction steals body heat quickly, which is one reason an off-ground hammock can outperform a floor tent.

Can a tarp or poncho really work as shelter?

Yes. A single tarp or a military poncho with a few tie-outs and some cordage can be rigged into a lean-to, an A-frame, or a low storm shelter in minutes, giving you a waterproof roof for a fraction of a tent's weight. The guide shows several pitches suited to different weather, plus how a poncho pulls double duty as rain gear and as an emergency shelter.

How do hammocks work as survival shelter?

A backpacking hammock with a full-coverage rainfly gets you off the wet, cold ground entirely and pitches between two trees where flat ground is scarce. An asymmetrical hammock lets you lie flatter and sleep more comfortably, while the fly creates a dry living space beneath it. Because ground contact drains heat, you pair the hammock with under-insulation such as an underquilt or pad in cold weather to stay warm.
