Curing and Smoking Meat Safely

Salt, nitrite, drying and smoke can be part of meat preservation, but they do not automatically make meat shelf-stable. Use a tested process appropriate to the specific meat and keep ordinary smoked meat refrigerated.

When refrigeration fails, do not rely on an improvised cure to rescue meat that has become unsafe. Plan preservation before an outage and use a food thermometer and validated instructions.

Frequently asked questions

How does salt preserve meat?

Salt reduces the water available to microbes but does not eliminate every pathogen. Use a tested recipe with measured quantities; ordinary brining or salting is not proof of shelf stability.

Why do cured meats need curing salt or nitrite?

Nitrite can help inhibit botulism in certain cured products. Use the exact curing salt specified in a validated recipe and weigh it accurately. Never substitute curing salts or estimate the amount.

What is the difference between cold and hot smoking?

Cold smoking does not cook meat and can leave it at unsafe temperatures. Do not improvise cold-smoking preservation. Hot smoking requires a safe cooking temperature for the meat type, verified with a thermometer; smoking alone does not make it shelf-stable.

How long does cured and smoked meat last unrefrigerated?

Refrigerate ordinary smoked meat within two hours of cooking, or within one hour in temperatures above 90°F. Only products made with a validated shelf-stable process should be stored without refrigeration. Do not rely on smell or appearance.

When the freezer is out, cold storage is the other half of the problem — see Off-Grid Refrigeration for keeping food cold without electricity.
