Frequently asked questions

How much rainwater can I actually collect?

Catchment is predictable: for every inch of rain falling on 1,000 square feet of roof you can harvest roughly 600 gallons before losses. Multiply your roof's footprint by local rainfall to size storage realistically. The guide includes catchment math, gutter and downspout sizing, and how to stage multiple barrels or a cistern so a few storms carry you through a dry spell.

Is harvested rainwater safe to drink?

Not straight from the tank. Rain picks up dust, pollen, bird droppings, and roofing residue on the way down, so it must be filtered and disinfected before drinking. A first-flush diverter removes the worst initial runoff, then filtration plus boiling or chemical treatment makes it potable. The guide details each step and points to the site's water-purification reference for treatment specifics.

What is a first-flush diverter and do I need one?

A first-flush diverter is a chamber that captures and discards the first several gallons of each rainfall — the dirtiest water that just washed contaminants off your roof — before clean water reaches the tank. It dramatically improves stored-water quality for any use. The guide shows how to build one from PVC, size it to your roof area, and drain it between storms.

Does roofing material affect rainwater quality?

Yes. Smooth, inert surfaces like metal roofing shed the cleanest water, while asphalt shingles, treated wood shakes, and old lead flashing can leach chemicals or particles into the catchment. The guide explains which roofing and gutter materials are best for potable collection, which to avoid, and how added filtration compensates when you cannot change the roof you already have.
