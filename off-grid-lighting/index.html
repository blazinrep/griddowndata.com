Frequently asked questions

What are the best ways to light a home without electricity?

The main options are fuel lamps burning oil or kerosene, candles, battery-powered LED lanterns and headlamps, and solar-charged lights. Each trades off brightness, run time, cost, and fire risk. A resilient plan layers several of them: solar and LED for everyday safe light, fuel lamps for long dark evenings, and candles as cheap backup, so that no single fuel shortage or equipment failure ever leaves the whole household in the dark.

Are oil and kerosene lamps safe to use indoors?

They can be, with care. Open-flame lamps consume oxygen and produce carbon monoxide and soot, so they require ventilation, a stable non-tip base set well away from anything flammable, and never being left burning unattended or while you sleep. The guide covers wick trimming, choosing the right fuel, and safe placement, because house fires and carbon-monoxide poisoning are leading preventable dangers during prolonged power outages.

How does solar lighting work off-grid?

Solar lights come in two forms: self-contained units with a built-in panel and battery that charge in daylight and glow at night, and separate fixtures run from a solar-charged battery bank. Self-contained lights are the simplest and cheapest, needing only sun exposure, while a battery-bank system can power brighter indoor fixtures. The guide explains sizing and placing them so your lighting refills itself each day without fuel or the grid.

How much lighting should I store for an emergency?

Plan for redundancy rather than a single lamp: enough fuel, spare wicks, batteries, and candles to light the rooms you actually use for several weeks, plus at least one headlamp per person for hands-free work. The guide helps you estimate burn times and battery life, rotate fuel and batteries so they stay fresh, and decide which rooms to prioritize so your lighting supplies last through a long outage.
