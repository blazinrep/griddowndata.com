Frequently asked questions

Why is sanitation a survival priority when the grid fails?

When sewage systems and running water stop, human waste quickly contaminates hands, food, and drinking water, spreading diseases such as cholera, dysentery, and hepatitis A. After many disasters, poor sanitation causes more illness and death than the original event. The guide treats waste disposal and handwashing as urgent physiological priorities, not afterthoughts, so preventable illness does not sweep through your household.

How do I build a simple emergency toilet?

The most reliable low-cost option is a five-gallon bucket fitted with a snap-on toilet seat lid, layered with a carbon material like sawdust, peat moss, or dry leaves after each use to control odor and begin composting. The guide covers lining the bucket, how much cover material to add, when to empty it, and how to cure or bury the contents safely.

Where should a latrine go relative to water?

A dug latrine, cat-hole, or slit trench should sit at least 200 feet, roughly 70 paces, downhill and away from any well, spring, stream, or pond so waste cannot leach into your drinking water. The guide gives depth guidelines, explains covering each deposit with soil to deter flies, and shows how to close and mark a latrine once it is full.

Can human waste be composted safely?

Yes, but it demands discipline. Properly managed humanure composting uses generous carbon cover material and a hot, active pile, followed by a long curing period, commonly a year or more, to destroy pathogens before the finished compost is used, and in many areas only on non-food landscaping. The guide explains temperatures, timing, and the sanitation limits so shortcuts do not spread disease.
