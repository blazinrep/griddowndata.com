A venomous bite is a race against swelling, and panic is the first thing that kills.

Snakebite treatment for a venomous bite is about slowing venom spread and reaching antivenom, not neutralizing venom in the field. The proven steps are to move away from the snake, keep the victim calm and still, remove jewelry and tight clothing before swelling begins, clean and loosely cover the bite, immobilize the limb near heart level, and mark the swelling edge with the time. You do not cut, suck, apply a tourniquet, or ice the wound. Frequently asked questions

What should I do first after a venomous snakebite?

Move out of the snake striking range, then keep the person as calm and still as possible, because a racing heart and moving muscles pump venom through the body faster. Remove rings, watches, and tight clothing before swelling starts, clean the bite with soap and water, cover it with a loose clean dressing, and get moving toward medical care and antivenom right away.

What should you never do to a snakebite?

Do not cut the wound or try to suck out venom, do not apply a tourniquet, and do not pack the bite in ice, because all of these concentrate venom in the tissue and cause worse local damage, sometimes costing fingers or limbs. Avoid alcohol and caffeine, and skip aspirin, ibuprofen, and naproxen, which thin the blood; acetaminophen is the safer choice for pain.

Should I keep the bitten limb raised or lowered?

Keep the bitten limb roughly at heart level and immobilized, splinting it as you would a fracture and limiting movement so muscle activity does not spread venom. Raising the limb high can speed venom toward the core, while letting it hang low worsens swelling, so a neutral, still position near heart level is the standard first-aid compromise while you get the victim to care.

How can I tell if a bite was venomous and how fast do I need help?

Not every bite injects venom, but you should treat any bite from an unknown or venomous snake as an emergency and reach antivenom as quickly as you can. Signs of envenomation include spreading pain and swelling, discoloration, tingling, nausea, and trouble breathing. Marking the swelling edge and the time helps clinicians judge how fast it is progressing when you arrive.
