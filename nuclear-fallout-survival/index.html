Frequently asked questions

What are the three principles of fallout protection?

Radiation protection comes down to distance, shielding, and time. Distance means putting as much space as possible between you and fallout particles, shielding means placing dense mass such as concrete, brick, packed earth, water, or books between you and the radiation, and time means staying sheltered while fallout is most intense. Together these three reduce your radiation dose far more than any single measure could on its own.

How long do I need to shelter after fallout?

Fallout radiation decays rapidly: it loses roughly 90 percent of its intensity within about the first three days and keeps dropping after that. The critical window is to shelter in place for at least the first 48 to 72 hours, when radiation is most dangerous, then follow official guidance before venturing out. The guide explains the decay curve and how to plan water, food, and air for that period.

What does potassium iodide actually protect?

Potassium iodide, or KI, saturates the thyroid gland with stable iodine so it cannot absorb radioactive iodine, protecting only that one gland and nothing else in the body. It does not shield you from external radiation or other fallout isotopes. It works best taken shortly before or within a few hours of exposure, and only when authorities recommend it, since needless use carries its own risks. The guide covers dosing cautions and its narrow role.

How do I improvise a fallout shelter at home?

Most homes already hold usable shelter: a basement corner or a windowless interior room on the lowest floor, surrounded by as much dense mass as you can stack, such as filled water containers, books, and furniture packed with earth or sand. Seal gaps against dust with plastic sheeting and tape, and set aside stored water, food, and a way to manage waste. The guide details maximizing shielding with whatever you already have on hand.
