Frequently asked questions

Why store whole grain instead of flour?

Whole grain berries sealed against moisture and oxygen stay good for 25 years or more, while ground flour oxidizes and goes rancid within a few months. Storing berries and milling only what you need keeps your long-term supply intact and your flour fresh. The guide explains packaging grain for the long haul and why a hand mill is the essential companion to any wheat stockpile.

What kind of hand mill grinds wheat into flour?

A manual grain mill uses either steel burrs or stone wheels to crush grain, adjustable from coarse cracked cereal to fine bread flour. Burr mills are versatile and affordable, while stone mills excel at fine flour. Well-built units like the Country Living mill are made to grind for decades. The guide compares mill types, mounting, and which handle oily seeds versus only dry grain.

How much effort does hand-milling really take?

Milling by hand is real work: producing enough fine flour for a single loaf of bread can take ten to twenty minutes of steady cranking, and finer flour requires more passes. The guide sets honest expectations, covers techniques to cut effort like double-grinding, flywheel add-ons, and pre-cracking grain, and explains how to batch a day's milling at once rather than grinding at every meal.

What grains and foods can a hand mill process?

Beyond wheat, most manual mills grind corn, rye, oats, rice, dried beans, and other dry grains and legumes into meal or flour, and some handle coffee and spices. Oily items like nuts or flax can gum up dry-grind burrs, so the guide notes which mills tolerate them. This versatility lets one tool turn a pantry of stored staples into varied, usable food.
