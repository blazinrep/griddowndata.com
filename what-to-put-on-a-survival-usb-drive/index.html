A hard drive full of unread PDFs is not a plan.

A survival USB drive needs five core categories to be useful in an actual emergency: a medical/first-aid reference, offline maps for your region, water and food guides, a communications reference, and an offline general knowledge base like Wikipedia. Most people stop at one or two categories and end up with a folder of PDFs they've never opened. The list below is what actually belongs on the drive, and why each category matters.

Downloading a pile of survival PDFs feels like preparation, but a drive organized around scenarios you can actually find in an emergency is what makes the difference. Here's the checklist, category by category — each one links to a deeper guide if you want the full breakdown.

How to actually organize it

  1. Start with the five core categories above — medical, water, food, communications, and a general knowledge base. Skipping any one of these leaves a real gap.
  2. Name folders by scenario, not by source. "Water — no power" is easier to find at 2am than "Downloads — misc PDFs."
  3. Add regional content last. Offline maps and navigation guides should match where you actually live or travel, not a generic national set.
  4. Test it before you need it. Open the drive on the actual device you'd use in an emergency — a laptop, a phone with an OTG adapter — and confirm every file opens without needing an app you don't already have installed.

Frequently asked questions

What should be on a survival USB drive?

At minimum: a medical/first-aid reference, offline maps for your region, a water purification and food storage guide, a communications reference (radio protocols and frequencies), and an offline copy of Wikipedia or a similar general knowledge base. Beyond that, add anything specific to your climate and situation — wood heat if you're in a cold region, solar wiring if you're building a power system, seed storage if you garden.

Is a PDF library enough, or do I need offline Wikipedia too?

PDFs cover known scenarios well, but they can't answer a question you didn't think to save a guide for. An offline Wikipedia mirror (via a ZIM file and a reader like Kiwix) fills that gap — it's searchable, general-purpose, and covers thousands of topics no single PDF library will include.

What format should the files be in?

PDF for guides and manuals (universal, opens on any device), ZIM for Wikipedia-style content (requires a reader like Kiwix), and standard map formats like GeoPDF or offline vector map packs for navigation. Avoid proprietary formats that need a specific paid app to open.

How much storage does a full survival archive need?

A solid medical, food, water, and repair PDF library runs a few gigabytes. A full offline Wikipedia mirror (text-only) is roughly 50–90 GB; with images it's several hundred GB. A 128GB drive comfortably fits a curated PDF library plus a text-only Wikipedia mirror and regional offline maps.

What do most people forget to include?

Offline maps for areas outside their immediate region, a general-purpose knowledge base like Wikipedia (most people only save topic-specific PDFs), and a plan for accessing the files without a charged laptop — a phone with a USB-C/OTG adapter is a common gap.
