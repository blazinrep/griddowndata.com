Diarrhea, not the disaster, is what fills the graves — and a cup of salt, sugar, and clean water is the fix.

Oral rehydration solution (ORS) is a precise mix of clean water, sugar, and salt that replaces the fluid and electrolytes lost to diarrhea and vomiting, the leading causes of death after many disasters. The standard home recipe dissolves six level teaspoons of sugar and half a level teaspoon of salt in one liter of clean, boiled-and-cooled water. Frequently asked questions

What is the exact oral rehydration solution recipe?

The widely used home recipe dissolves six level teaspoons of sugar and one half level teaspoon of table salt in one liter of clean drinking water, ideally boiled and then cooled first. Accuracy matters, because too much salt or sugar can actually worsen dehydration, so the guide stresses level measures and clean water. The solution is sipped steadily and replaces the fluid and electrolytes lost to diarrhea far better than plain water alone.

Why does ORS work better than drinking plain water?

Diarrhea strips the body of water along with sodium and potassium, and plain water can neither replace those salts nor be absorbed efficiently by an irritated gut. The modest amount of sugar in ORS activates a sodium-glucose co-transport mechanism in the intestinal wall that pulls both salt and water back into the body. The guide explains this plainly so you understand why the sugar-to-salt ratio is essential rather than optional.

How much oral rehydration solution should I give?

After each loose stool, an adult generally needs a large glass, roughly 200 to 400 millilitres, and a young child about a quarter to half that amount, sipped slowly rather than gulped down. The aim is to keep pace with ongoing fluid losses. The guide gives age-based guidance and stresses continuing to offer small, frequent sips even when someone is vomiting, until the diarrhea eases and normal drinking resumes.

When is dehydration too severe for home treatment?

Warning signs that demand urgent professional care include sunken eyes, no urination for many hours, extreme lethargy or confusion, inability to keep any fluids down, and a weak, rapid pulse or other signs of shock, all especially dangerous in infants and the elderly. The guide helps you recognize the line between mild dehydration you can manage with ORS and severe dehydration that needs intravenous fluids and medical attention as soon as it can be reached.
